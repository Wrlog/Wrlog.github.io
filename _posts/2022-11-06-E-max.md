## How the Emax model got here

Pharmacodynamics has to answer a question pharmacokinetics cannot: given the
time course of drug in plasma, what is the time course of effect? The models
in routine use today were built up over about forty years, each one added
because the previous one could not explain something observed.

### Levy, 1966: effect declines linearly, concentration does not

Levy noticed something that looks paradoxical. Plasma concentrations fall
exponentially, yet the measured effect of many drugs falls off in a straight
line. Writing $E$ for effect, this behaves as

$$
E = E_0 - k \cdot m \cdot t
$$

where $k$ is the elimination rate constant and $m$ is the slope of the
effect against log concentration. The apparent contradiction dissolves once
you notice that effect is roughly linear in *log* concentration over the
middle of its range, and log concentration falls linearly in time. The product
$k \cdot m$ therefore sets how fast effect decays.

The limitation is in that phrase "over the middle of its range": the
relationship only holds between roughly 20% and 80% of maximum effect. Outside
that window it breaks down, because effect saturates and concentration does not.

### Wagner, 1968: borrowing the Hill equation

The fix was to describe the saturation directly. Wagner applied the equation
A.V. Hill had derived in 1910 for oxygen binding to haemoglobin, which follows
from the same mass-action reasoning as drug binding to a receptor:

$$
E(t) = \frac{E_{max} \cdot C(t)^{\gamma}}{EC_{50}^{\gamma} + C(t)^{\gamma}}
$$

$E_{max}$ is the maximum achievable effect, $EC_{50}$ the concentration giving
half of it, and $\gamma$ the Hill coefficient controlling how steeply effect
turns on. This is the Emax model, and it removed the 20-80% restriction: it is
valid across the whole concentration range because saturation is built in.

### Segre and Sheiner: effect lags concentration

The Emax model as written assumes peak effect coincides with peak
concentration. For most drugs it does not — effect lags, and plotting effect
against concentration traces a loop rather than a curve (hysteresis).

Segre's idea, which Sheiner and colleagues made standard in 1979 with
d-tubocurarine, was to interpose a hypothetical **effect compartment**: a
small, kinetically distinct space that the drug equilibrates into with its own
rate constant. Effect is still given by the Emax equation, but driven by the
concentration in that compartment rather than in plasma. The delay is then a
distribution delay, and by construction it does not perturb the plasma PK.

### Ariëns, Nagashima, Jusko: when the delay is not distribution

An effect compartment explains delays of minutes to hours. It cannot explain
delays of days, and it cannot explain a response that continues changing after
the drug has gone.

Those delays arise when the drug does not act on the measured quantity at all,
but on its **turnover**. Ariëns had argued for indirect mechanisms as early as
1954; Nagashima and colleagues built the first quantitative example in 1969,
for warfarin — which does not alter clotting time directly but suppresses
synthesis of the clotting factors that determine it. Dayneka and Jusko later
organised these into four canonical models, according to whether the drug
inhibits or stimulates production ($k_{in}$) or loss ($k_{out}$):

$$
\frac{dR}{dt} = k_{in} \cdot H_1(C) - k_{out} \cdot H_2(C) \cdot R
$$

where $H_1$ and $H_2$ are Emax-type functions of concentration and $R_0$ is
the baseline. Here the time course of the response is set by the turnover of
the biomarker, not by the pharmacokinetics — which is why washout after
stopping the drug can take far longer than the drug's own half-life.

One distinguishing feature is useful in practice: for an effect-compartment
model the time to peak effect is independent of dose, whereas for an indirect
response model it shifts later as dose increases, because concentrations stay
above $EC_{50}$ for longer.

### And when even that is too fast

Some systems show no change at all for a period after dosing, despite high
concentrations — signal transduction cascades, for instance. Chains of
**transit compartments** are the usual device for these, adding a controllable
delay with few extra parameters.

Everything after this point is elaboration on the same four ideas: saturation,
distribution delay, turnover, and transduction delay.

## Reading an Emax curve

The Emax model is worth understanding graphically as well as algebraically,
because the two parameters people most often conflate are separated by it:

- **Potency** is $EC_{50}$, read off the **x-axis**. It says how much drug you
  need.
- **Efficacy** is $E_{max}$, read off the **y-axis**. It says how much effect
  you can get at all.

These are independent, and neither predicts the other. A very potent drug with
low efficacy reaches its modest ceiling at a low dose. A drug of low potency
with high efficacy needs a lot of drug but can drive the response further. The
practical consequence: comparing two drugs on $EC_{50}$ alone tells you about
dose size, not about what the drug can achieve.
