## How the Emax model got here

PK gives the time course of drug in plasma. PD asks what the time course of
effect is. The models used routinely today were built up over about forty
years, each one added because the previous one couldn't explain something
people were seeing.

### Levy, 1966: effect declines linearly, concentration does not

Levy noticed that plasma concentrations fall exponentially while the measured
effect of many drugs falls off in a straight line. Writing $E$ for effect:

$$
E = E_0 - k \cdot m \cdot t
$$

where $k$ is the elimination rate constant and $m$ is the slope of the
effect against log concentration. This works because effect is roughly linear
in *log* concentration over the middle of its range, and log concentration
falls linearly with time, so $k \cdot m$ sets how fast effect decays.

It only holds over that middle range, roughly 20% to 80% of maximum effect.
Outside it the relationship breaks down, because effect saturates and
concentration doesn't.

### Wagner, 1968: borrowing the Hill equation

Wagner described the saturation directly, using the equation A.V. Hill had
derived in 1910 for oxygen binding to haemoglobin, which follows from the same
mass-action reasoning as drug binding to a receptor:

$$
E(t) = \frac{E_{max} \cdot C(t)^{\gamma}}{EC_{50}^{\gamma} + C(t)^{\gamma}}
$$

$E_{max}$ is the maximum achievable effect, $EC_{50}$ the concentration giving
half of it, and $\gamma$ the Hill coefficient controlling how steeply effect
turns on. This is the Emax model. Because saturation is built in, it holds
across the whole concentration range and the 20-80% restriction goes away.

### Segre and Sheiner: effect lags concentration

As written, the Emax model assumes peak effect happens at peak concentration.
For most drugs it doesn't. Effect lags, and a plot of effect against
concentration traces a loop instead of a curve (hysteresis).

Segre's idea, which Sheiner and colleagues made standard in 1979 with
d-tubocurarine, was to add a hypothetical effect compartment: a small,
kinetically separate space that the drug equilibrates into with its own rate
constant. Effect is still given by the Emax equation, but driven by the
concentration in that compartment instead of plasma. The delay is then a
distribution delay, and by construction it doesn't change the plasma PK.

### Ariëns, Nagashima, Jusko: when the delay is not distribution

An effect compartment explains delays of minutes to hours. It can't explain
delays of days, or a response that keeps changing after the drug is gone.

Those delays happen when the drug acts on the turnover of the measured
quantity rather than on the quantity itself. Ariëns argued for indirect
mechanisms as early as 1954, and Nagashima and colleagues built the first
quantitative example in 1969 for warfarin, which doesn't change clotting time
directly but suppresses synthesis of the clotting factors that determine it.
Dayneka and Jusko later organised these into four basic models, depending on
whether the drug inhibits or stimulates production ($k_{in}$) or loss
($k_{out}$):

$$
\frac{dR}{dt} = k_{in} \cdot H_1(C) - k_{out} \cdot H_2(C) \cdot R
$$

where $H_1$ and $H_2$ are Emax-type functions of concentration and $R_0$ is
the baseline. The time course of the response is now set by the turnover of
the biomarker rather than the PK, which is why washout after stopping the drug
can take much longer than the drug's own half-life.

One way to tell the two apart: in an effect-compartment model the time to peak
effect doesn't depend on dose, while in an indirect response model it moves
later as dose goes up, because concentrations stay above $EC_{50}$ for longer.

### Transit compartments

Some systems show no change at all for a while after dosing, even at high
concentrations (signal transduction cascades, for example). The usual approach
is a chain of transit compartments, which adds an adjustable delay with few
extra parameters.

Most PK/PD models since then build on these four ideas: saturation,
distribution delay, turnover and transduction delay.

## Reading an Emax curve

The curve is a good way to keep potency and efficacy apart, since they get
mixed up a lot:

- Potency is $EC_{50}$, read off the x-axis: how much drug you need.
- Efficacy is $E_{max}$, read off the y-axis: how much effect you can get at
  all.

They're independent and neither predicts the other. A very potent drug with
low efficacy reaches its modest ceiling at a low dose. A low-potency drug with
high efficacy needs a lot of drug but can push the response further. So
comparing two drugs on $EC_{50}$ alone tells you about dose size and nothing
about what the drug can achieve.
