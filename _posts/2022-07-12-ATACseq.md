## What ATAC-seq measures

ATAC-seq (Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin with sequencing) tells you
which parts of the genome are physically open.

The method is simple. A hyperactive Tn5 transposase is loaded with sequencing
adapters and added to nuclei. Tn5 can only cut where DNA isn't tightly wrapped
around nucleosomes, and it inserts the adapters as it cuts. Open regions get
fragmented and tagged, closed chromatin doesn't, and after sequencing the read
density is a map of accessibility.

This is useful because a lot of regulation happens in accessible chromatin.
Promoters, enhancers and active transcription-factor binding sites are in open
regions, so an accessibility map narrows down where to look for regulatory
elements a lot, and you don't need to know in advance which factor you're
after.

## How it differs from ChIP-seq

The two get mentioned together but answer different questions:

| | ATAC-seq | ChIP-seq |
|---|---|---|
| **Question** | Which regions are open? | Where does *this specific* protein bind? |
| **Needs an antibody** | No | Yes, one per target |
| **Input required** | Very low (thousands of cells) | Typically millions of cells |
| **Scope** | Genome-wide accessibility, factor-agnostic | One factor or histone mark at a time |

In practice ATAC-seq often comes first to show where to look, and ChIP-seq
follows to show which protein is bound there. The low input requirement is
also why ATAC-seq became the standard for single-cell chromatin work.

## Quality control

Three checks tell you whether a library is usable:

- Fragment size distribution. A good library shows clear periodicity: a
  sub-nucleosomal peak below ~100 bp, then peaks near 200 and 400 bp for mono-
  and di-nucleosomes. If that ladder is missing, the digestion was over- or
  under-done.
- TSS enrichment. Reads should pile up sharply at transcription start sites.
  A flat profile means the signal is mostly background.
- Mitochondrial contamination. Mitochondrial DNA has no nucleosomes, so Tn5
  cuts it freely and it can take up a large share of reads. Omni-ATAC and
  similar protocol revisions were largely designed to reduce this.

Peaks are then usually called with MACS2. Its peak model was designed for
ChIP-seq, so the assumptions don't carry over perfectly; shifting reads to
centre on the Tn5 insertion site is a standard correction.

## Deep learning

Accessibility data suits sequence models well. The input is a one-dimensional
string over a four-letter alphabet, the output is a per-position signal, and
there's a lot of data. Convolutional models more or less automatically learn
motif-like filters in their first layer, which is why this combination took
off early.

The main caution is the one in the Whalen et al. paper below. Genomic data is
highly structured, and a random train/test split puts adjacent or homologous
regions on both sides, so the reported accuracy is partly measuring
memorisation. Splitting by chromosome is the usual minimum precaution. It's
the same kind of leakage you get from any careless split.

## Reading list

ATAC-seq

1. Buenrostro JD, et al. Transposition of native chromatin for fast and
   sensitive epigenomic profiling of open chromatin, DNA-binding proteins and
   nucleosome position. *Nat Methods.* 2013. The original method paper.
2. Yan F, et al. From reads to insight: a hitchhiker's guide to ATAC-seq data
   analysis. *Genome Biol.* 2020. The most practical walkthrough of the full
   analysis.
3. Ou J, et al. ATACseqQC: a Bioconductor package for post-alignment quality
   assessment of ATAC-seq data. *BMC Genomics.* 2018.
4. Landt SG, et al. ChIP-seq guidelines and practices of the ENCODE and
   modENCODE consortia. *Genome Res.* 2012. Still the reference for what good
   quality control looks like.
5. Zhang Y, et al. Model-based Analysis of ChIP-Seq (MACS). *Genome Biol.* 2008.

Deep learning in genomics

6. Eraslan G, et al. Deep learning: new computational modelling techniques for
   genomics. *Nat Rev Genet.* 2019.
7. Zou J, et al. A primer on deep learning in genomics. *Nat Genet.* 2019.
8. Whalen S, et al. Navigating the pitfalls of applying machine learning in
   genomics. *Nat Rev Genet.* 2022. Worth reading alongside the others rather
   than after them.
