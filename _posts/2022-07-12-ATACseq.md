## What ATAC-seq measures

ATAC-seq (Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin with sequencing) answers
one question: **which parts of the genome are physically open right now?**

The method is elegantly direct. A hyperactive Tn5 transposase is loaded with
sequencing adapters and let loose on nuclei. Tn5 can only cut where DNA is not
wrapped tightly in nucleosomes, and it inserts the adapters at the same moment
it cuts. Regions that are open get fragmented and tagged; regions packed into
closed chromatin do not. Sequence the result, and read density becomes a map of
accessibility.

That matters because accessibility is largely where regulation happens.
Promoters, enhancers and active transcription-factor binding sites sit in open
chromatin, so an accessibility map narrows the search space for regulatory
elements enormously — without needing to know in advance which factor you are
looking for.

## How it differs from ChIP-seq

The two are often mentioned together and answer different questions:

| | ATAC-seq | ChIP-seq |
|---|---|---|
| **Question** | Which regions are open? | Where does *this specific* protein bind? |
| **Needs an antibody** | No | Yes, one per target |
| **Input required** | Very low (thousands of cells) | Typically millions of cells |
| **Scope** | Genome-wide accessibility, factor-agnostic | One factor or histone mark at a time |

The practical consequence is that ATAC-seq is usually the first experiment —
it tells you where to look — and ChIP-seq the follow-up that tells you who is
sitting there. The low input requirement is also why ATAC-seq, not ChIP-seq,
became the standard for single-cell chromatin work.

## Quality control that actually matters

Three checks separate a usable ATAC-seq library from a wasted one:

- **Fragment size distribution.** A good library shows clear periodicity: a
  sub-nucleosomal peak below ~100 bp, then peaks near 200 and 400 bp
  corresponding to mono- and di-nucleosomes. Losing that ladder means the
  digestion was over- or under-done.
- **TSS enrichment.** Reads should pile up sharply at transcription start
  sites. A flat profile indicates the signal is mostly background.
- **Mitochondrial contamination.** Mitochondrial DNA has no nucleosomes, so
  Tn5 attacks it freely and it can swallow a large share of reads. Omni-ATAC
  and similar protocol revisions exist largely to suppress this.

Peak calling is then usually done with MACS2, though the peak model was
designed for ChIP-seq and the assumptions do not transfer perfectly — read
shifting to centre on the Tn5 insertion site is a standard correction.

## Where deep learning comes in

Accessibility data is a natural target for sequence models: the input is a
one-dimensional string over a four-letter alphabet, the output is a
per-position signal, and there is a great deal of it. Convolutional models
learn motif-like filters in their first layer more or less automatically, which
is why this pairing took off early.

The caution worth keeping is the one the Whalen et al. paper below is about:
genomic data is highly structured, and a random train/test split will place
adjacent or homologous regions on both sides. The reported accuracy is then
measuring memorisation, not generalisation. Splitting by chromosome is the
usual minimum precaution — the same class of mistake as any other leakage
through a careless split.

## Reading list

**ATAC-seq**

1. Buenrostro JD, et al. Transposition of native chromatin for fast and
   sensitive epigenomic profiling of open chromatin, DNA-binding proteins and
   nucleosome position. *Nat Methods.* 2013. — the original method paper.
2. Yan F, et al. From reads to insight: a hitchhiker's guide to ATAC-seq data
   analysis. *Genome Biol.* 2020. — the most practical end-to-end walkthrough.
3. Ou J, et al. ATACseqQC: a Bioconductor package for post-alignment quality
   assessment of ATAC-seq data. *BMC Genomics.* 2018.
4. Landt SG, et al. ChIP-seq guidelines and practices of the ENCODE and
   modENCODE consortia. *Genome Res.* 2012. — still the reference for what
   good quality control looks like.
5. Zhang Y, et al. Model-based Analysis of ChIP-Seq (MACS). *Genome Biol.* 2008.

**Deep learning in genomics**

6. Eraslan G, et al. Deep learning: new computational modelling techniques for
   genomics. *Nat Rev Genet.* 2019.
7. Zou J, et al. A primer on deep learning in genomics. *Nat Genet.* 2019.
8. Whalen S, et al. Navigating the pitfalls of applying machine learning in
   genomics. *Nat Rev Genet.* 2022. — read this one alongside any of the
   others, not after.
